Blessing Ngozi Uzor

Researcher in language model evaluation · African Institute for Mathematical Sciences, Cameroon

I study how language models behave for the people they serve, with a focus on African languages and cultures. I design benchmarks, combine human evaluation with LLM-as-a-judge, and look for the failures that standard tests miss. I have also worked on changing how small models answer without retraining them. How do we make LLMs multilingual while genuinely promoting multiculturalism, and how do we measure that? I also work on AI safety across countries and cultures, and I am interested in what these questions mean for robots that use language models to decide what to say and do.

Why

Language is culture. Language is memory. Language is home.

I was born in Nigeria, where more than 500 languages coexist within a single nation. We do not just speak languages; we move between them, think through them, dream inside them. Igbo proverbs at breakfast make us hear directly from our forefathers, ancient wisdom. Nigerian Pidgin in the market turns strangers into instant understanding. English in school opens institutional doors. Hausa on the radio binds regions together across distance and difference.

Code-switching is not confusion. It is fluency in being Nigerian.

Multilingualism, multiculturalism, code-switching—these are not features of life here. They are life itself. They shape how we love, argue, trade, pray, joke, and mourn. They are not background context. They are the structure of everyday thought.

Yet much of the technology built for the world was not built with this reality in mind. Language models trained on English monolingualism encounter Nigerian multilingualism and fail to see us clearly. They stumble over our names. They flatten our idioms. They miss the cultural knowledge carried in how we speak. In their logic, linguistic richness becomes noise.

That failure is not neutral. It is a form of erasure.

This is not only a technical problem. It is a question of what language systems choose to see and what they erase.

This is the gap that drives my work. I cannot accept a future where African languages are treated as edge cases in systems meant to represent the world. I want to build language technologies that recognize people as they are, not as simplified versions shaped for convenience. Systems that understand African languages not as anomalies, but as knowledge. As structure. As intelligence.

Because diversity and inclusion are not abstract ideals. They are the difference between being seen and being erased.

They are the hundreds of languages that carry people's histories, identities, and ways of seeing the world.

And they are us.

"Culture is not a dataset. It is a living inheritance, passed from voice to voice, and no model will ever be trained enough to replace what a people carry in their bones. The sacred culture of the Igbo people will endure in its full authenticity long after every model trained on its shadow has been forgotten. What a people know in their bones cannot be benchmarked. The attempt to measure that gap is where this work begins."

Blessing Ngozi Uzor
